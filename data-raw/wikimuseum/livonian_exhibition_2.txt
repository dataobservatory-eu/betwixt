<center><big>'''Headgear'''</big><br />exhibition in the [[Vikipeedia:GLAM/Traditional Livonian Clothing|Traditional Livonian Clothing]]</center>

This room of the virtual exhibition features 23 Livonian headdresses from the collections of the National Museum of Finland and the Estonian National Museum. The vast majority are women’s headdresses.

The first is a highly unusual crown made from strips of paper. According to the Finnish ethnographer A. O. Heikel, young unmarried women wore such crowns as decorative accessories when attending dance evenings. This is followed by the headdresses of married women. The first eight of these are typical of the Livonian-inhabited areas, where the decoration was concentrated on the back part of the cap. The adornments included colourful silk ribbons, fragments of brocade bands, glass beads, and colourful embroidery. The use of such caps was already documented during an ethnographic expedition to the Livonian Coast in 1846.

The next group comprises married women’s caps that reflect various mid- and late-19th-century fashion trends in headdress design. These are more often made of brightly coloured fabrics and only rarely decorated with embroidery. It seems that these headdresses are chronologically later than the previous group. None of them are unique to the Livonians—similar headdresses are found across a wider region.

Kerchiefs were more popular in Livonian territories than in many other parts of Latvia. They were worn by both young women and married women. Silk and cotton square kerchiefs were tied on the head or draped over the shoulders on top of the shirt. The earliest known kerchiefs featured geometric patterns, typically with intersecting coloured stripes forming tartan-style grids. The display of women’s headdresses concludes with a tulle headpiece decorated with artificial flowers, of the kind worn by married women in the final decade of the 19th century.

Finally, we can see two men’s headdresses. The first is a classic tall top hat made of dark felted wool, with relatively narrow brims. It belongs to an earlier stage of clothing development and such hats were worn along the Livonian Coast in the first half of the 19th century. The second is a distinctive cotton cap with a visor. These types of caps became part of men’s dress from the 1840s onward.
